ครับ and ค่ะ: Thai Polite Particles and Pronouns Explained

Updated October 6, 2026 · Politeness for learners of Thai

Thai doesn't change its verbs to sound polite. Instead, politeness lives in small words: the particles ครับ (khráp) and ค่ะ (khâ) at the end of a sentence, and the pronouns you choose for "I" and "you". Leave them out with a stranger and you sound abrupt. Use the wrong one and you may sound like you learned Thai from someone of a different gender. This guide explains how polite particles work, which pronouns to use, and how to sound respectful in everyday Thai.

Romanization here marks tones with accents (á high, à low, â falling, ǎ rising, unmarked mid), so it may look different from Thai street signs.

Polite particles: ครับ, ค่ะ and คะ

Particle Romanization Who says it When
ครับ khráp Male speakers Statements and questions
ค่ะ khâ Female speakers Statements, and as "yes"
คะ khá Female speakers Questions, and after a name when calling someone

The particle depends on the speaker's gender, not the listener's. A man says ครับ to everyone, and a woman says ค่ะ or คะ to everyone. In casual speech, ครับ is often pronounced kháp.

These particles go at the end of almost any sentence to make it polite: สวัสดีครับ (sà-wàt-dii khráp, "Hello," from a man), ขอบคุณค่ะ (khàwp-khun khâ, "Thank you," from a woman). On their own, ครับ and ค่ะ also mean "yes" or "I see", and Thais use them constantly to show they're listening.

The difference between ค่ะ and คะ matters for female speakers. ค่ะ (falling tone) ends statements: ได้ค่ะ ("Sure"). คะ (high tone) ends questions: เท่าไหร่คะ ("How much?"). After นะ, women say นะคะ (ná-khá), which softens a request or statement.

Pronouns for "I" and "you"

Pronoun Romanization Meaning Use
ผม phǒm I (male) Polite, everyday for men
ดิฉัน dì-chǎn I (female) Formal: presentations, business, official settings
ฉัน chǎn I Everyday for women; also common in songs and writing
หนู nǔu I (literally "mouse") Girls and young women to elders
เรา rao we, I Friendly and casual
คุณ khun you Polite "you", and a title before first names
พี่ phîi older sibling Anyone older than you, as "you" or about yourself
น้อง náwng younger sibling Anyone younger than you

Two things make Thai pronouns feel different from English. First, Thais often drop pronouns entirely when context is clear, so ไปไหนครับ ("Where are you going?") has no "you" at all. Second, kinship words like พี่ and น้อง work as pronouns. A café worker might call you พี่, and you might call them น้อง.

คุณ is also used as a polite title before first names: คุณสมชาย ("Khun Somchai"). Thais usually use first names, not surnames, even in formal settings.

When to use polite particles

  • Strangers, shop staff, taxi drivers: always.
  • Colleagues and your boss: always at work, especially with seniors.
  • Elders, teachers, monks: always, often with more formal vocabulary.
  • Close friends your age: often dropped, or replaced with casual particles like จ้ะ (jâ) or จ้า (jâa), which sound warm and friendly.
  • Presentations and formal speech: always, together with ดิฉัน or ผม.

When in doubt, add the particle. Using ครับ or ค่ะ "too much" is never a problem, while leaving it out with strangers sounds blunt.

Beyond particles: formal Thai and royal vocabulary

Thai also has formal vocabulary for official speech and writing, such as รับประทาน (ráp-prà-thaan, "to eat", formal) instead of กิน (kin). There's a special vocabulary for speaking to or about monks, and an elaborate set of words for the royal family, called ราชาศัพท์ (raa-chaa-sàp). Learners don't need to use ราชาศัพท์, but you'll hear it in the news whenever the royal family is mentioned.

Common mistakes

  • Men saying ค่ะ. Many learners study with female teachers and copy their particles. Men should say ครับ.
  • Mixing up ค่ะ and คะ. Women use ค่ะ for statements and คะ for questions. เท่าไหร่ค่ะ with a falling tone sounds off.
  • Repeating คุณ in every sentence. Thais drop pronouns when the meaning is clear. Saying คุณ over and over sounds like a translated textbook.
  • Dropping particles with strangers. ขอบคุณ alone to a shop assistant is fine but curt. ขอบคุณครับ or ขอบคุณค่ะ is the norm.
  • Using กู and มึง. These "I" and "you" words are used between very close friends or as insults. You'll hear them in movies, but avoid them.

FAQ

Do I always need to say ครับ or ค่ะ?

With strangers, staff, colleagues, elders and anyone senior, yes. With close friends your age, you can drop them. When unsure, include them.

What's the difference between ค่ะ and คะ?

Both are female polite particles. ค่ะ (khâ, falling tone) ends statements and means "yes". คะ (khá, high tone) ends questions and follows a name when you call someone, as in พี่คะ ("Excuse me," to an older person).

Can a man say ค่ะ?

Traditionally, men say ครับ and women say ค่ะ/คะ. Some gay and transgender Thai speakers choose the particle that matches their identity, and that's accepted. A learner who uses the "wrong" one by accident will just sound like they copied their teacher.

What does นะ add?

นะ (ná) softens a sentence, adding warmth or gentle insistence, a bit like "okay?" or "you know". Women say นะคะ and men say นะครับ: รอแป๊บนึงนะคะ ("Just a moment, okay?").

What is ราชาศัพท์?

It's the special royal vocabulary used when speaking to or about members of the Thai royal family. You'll hear it in the news and at ceremonies, but learners aren't expected to use it in daily life.
